    Step 3: health-checked the tracking data done

    Scripts: analysis/atd_arrays.py (fast reader, cache in git-ignored data/cache/) and analysis/02_tracking_health.py. Aggregates only came back to Claude.

    • The public window is the best-tracked 5 minutes of the match. Ball visible 88% there; typical 5-minute block 60%, worst 20%. Any method looks better there than it will on the real test.
    • Ball blackouts are long. 150 gaps, median 10.7 s; 85% of missing frames sit in gaps over 10 s; almost no 1–3 s gaps. Interpolation will barely help.
    • A missing ball partly means a stoppage. Players move at 0.98 m/s during long blackouts against 2.00 m/s normally.
    • The ball is still visible on 66% of dead-ball frames (public window). Dead balls show slower ball (40% under 2 m/s vs 15–18%), ball off the pitch (11% vs ~0%) and walking players (1.2 vs 2.0–2.5 m/s), but each overlaps with live play.
    • Ball speed field is missing on 92% of visible frames; we compute it from positions instead.
    • Ball positions are trustworthy when present: 0.3% impossible jumps.
    • Players: 20.5 visible per frame; one team shows more than 11 in 13% of frames; about 11 new track segments per minute. Reason about teams, not individuals.

    Design consequences: combined-evidence dead-ball detector; context reasoning plus X for long blackouts; external data to measure progress.

  33. 6 Nov 2026

    Researched how possession is defined (your question)

    • Clarification: the Step 4b difference is DFL's labelling style vs our ground truth (from Metrica's corrected tracking), not LaLiga vs Bundesliga.
    • No official DFL or Metrica wording is public. Tracking providers' possession columns "sometimes are not switched as expected" (databallpy docs).
    • Bundesliga research uses a touch-based definition: possession "begins when a player touches the ball, and continues until another player touches the ball" (Link et al., PLOS ONE). That explains DFL's many brief possessions.
    • Industry standard is control-based (Opta / Stats Perform): "A possession is ended by the opposition gaining control of the ball"; tackles and interceptions only start a sequence "if followed by a controlled action such as a pass or dribble". Matches CHALLENGE.md and our D16.

Decisions

What we decided and why. If we change one, we add a new decision instead of deleting the old one.

D1Design for spellF1, not per-frame accuracy

Our main enemy is fragmentation: splitting one real possession into several. A system that changes label rarely and at the right moment beats one that is slightly more accurate frame by frame.

Why: spellF1 decides the ranking. The repo's own baselines show macroF1 barely moves while spellF1 changes five-fold.

D2The public 5 minutes are for understanding and format checks only

We look at them to learn what counts as a possession. We never use their score to choose between versions or settings.

Why: it breaks the rules, and with only 17 possessions it picks the wrong answer anyway (smooth125 wins there but loses on the full match).

D3Every setting is tuned on external data pending organiser answer

We will find open tracking data from other matches. Whether a value may be set from football reasoning alone is one of our questions to the organisers.

Why: required by the organisers' clarification email.

D4Claude does not see raw match data pending organiser answer

Claude writes the code; it runs on your machine; only what we need for decisions (pass/fail, counts) is shared, and only once the organisers confirm that is fine.

Why: the rules forbid uploading the data to AI services, and Claude is a cloud AI service.

D5Match data lives only in data/, never in a public repo

That folder is git-ignored. Our submission repo must be public, so this matters at every commit.

Why: redistributing the data breaks the terms we signed.

D6One step at a time

Finish a step, explain it, record it here, then move on.

Why: you need to understand every part, both to win and to write the technical document.

D7Claude may run local scripts that return only scores and counts

Agreed on 6 Nov for Step 2. Scripts read the tracking on your laptop; only scoreboards and summary counts come back, never raw tracking rows.

Why: lets us move while the organisers answer question 3. Revisit when they reply.

D8Reproducibility is checked by regenerating files and diffing them

Rebuild the output from scratch and confirm git shows no change, exactly as we did with the baselines.

Why: the organisers may rerun our repo, and the file must match byte for byte.

D9Build on tactical tracking and Smart Tagging; park the broadcast feed agreed 6 Nov

Core: tactical ATD. Helper: Smart Tagging. Optional: tactical homography and video. Parked: everything broadcast.

Why: the broadcast feed has no frame mapping to the tactical timeline, and syncing it would eat days we need for the dead-ball and transition problems. Revisit only if we hit a ceiling.

D10Detect dead balls by combining evidence, not one rule

Ball off the pitch, ball slow, players walking, a long ball blackout and Smart Tagging set pieces, weighed together.

Why: Step 3 showed every one of these points the right way but overlaps with live play, and the ball is still visible on two-thirds of dead-ball frames.

D11Compute ball speed from positions

Distance moved over 5 frames (0.2 s), in m/s. The tracking's own ball speed field is ignored.

Why: that field is missing on 92% of frames where the ball is visible.

D12IDSSE/DFL is our main tuning data agreed 6 Nov

Metrica sample data as a second check once the organisers confirm; SkillCorner as backup; PFF parked.

Why: IDSSE has frame-by-frame possession and ball-in-play labels built the same way as our ground truth, at the same 25 fps, under a clear CC-BY 4.0 licence.

D13Make external data messy before tuning on it agreed 6 Nov

Remove the ball in long blackouts, mostly during slow play, to resemble our match's tracking.

Why: a method tuned on clean tracking may fail on our automatic, uncorrected tracking. Copies the gap pattern from the tracking, never from the ground truth or video; we will ask the organisers to confirm.

D14Measure progress by leave-one-match-out on the Bundesliga data

Tune on 6 matches, score on the 7th, rotate through all 7, average. The public 5 minutes are never used to compare versions.

Why: honest measurement on football the model has not seen, within the rules. Step 3 showed the public window is the easiest 5 minutes of our match.

D15Keep a "merge very brief possessions" switch, decided by football reasoning

The DFL labels may count more brief possessions than our ground truth. Whether to merge them is settled in Step 5 from the challenge's own rule (duels and deflections don't change possession), not by score.

Why: Step 4b showed a likely but unproven difference in granularity, and we may not settle it with our ground truth.

D16A brief spell counts only if it contains a deliberate action

Controlled pass or carry, however short. A ricochet off a player doesn't count. Settles D15: merge brief spells without such an action.

Why: matches the challenge's "controlled possession" rule and keeps real 0.8 s possessions while dropping deflections. Your call, 6 Nov.

D17Fouls make the ball dead at the moment of the foul

Not at the whistle. To be verified as the convention in the Bundesliga labels.

Why: how tracking providers usually mark ball status. Your call, 6 Nov.

D18Build both an "X when unsure" and an "always guess" version

Compare them on the Bundesliga data with leave-one-match-out, never on the public 5 minutes.

Why: X is free inside possessions on spellF1 but costs macroF1; measuring beats guessing. Your call, 6 Nov.

D19Throw-in team from two clues combined

The player at the touchline with the ball, cross-checked with the team that didn't have it before it went out.

Why: Smart Tagging never names the throw-in team. Your call, 6 Nov.

D20Three-layer model: features, frame judge, match-flow referee agreed 6 Nov

Layer 1: football features per frame from tracking and tagging. Layer 2: gradient-boosted trees give H/A/D probabilities per frame, trained on the messy Bundesliga matches. Layer 3: an HMM with Viterbi turns them into clean possessions, enforcing the rulebook (switches are costly, dead balls last seconds, restarts follow stoppages).

Why: trees suit 7 matches of data, are fast, deterministic and explainable; the state model is the cure for flicker. Deep learning would need far more data.

D21The test bench is the judge

Every idea is scored on the 14 degraded Bundesliga halves with the official metric code; we keep a change only if it wins there.

Why: it reproduces the baselines' pattern on our match (picks ±2 s like the full-match results), where the public 5 minutes picked the wrong window.

D22Un-freeze the Bundesliga ball during stoppages

The nearest player collects and carries the ball, which then moves to the restart spot over the last 2 seconds. Stoppages are found from the DFL's own labels.

Why: DFL parks the ball during stoppages, which never happens in our tracking; models learned that shortcut. Checked against our whole match's overall statistics, no answers used.

D23Only use clues whose behaviour matches between bench and our match

Before trusting a clue, compare its overall distribution on the bench and on our full match (no answers needed). Ball-speed clues are off until they match.

Why: a clue that behaves differently on the bench teaches the model something false about our match. Step 7 found two such traps.

D24More training data only if the learning curve is still rising

Train on 2 to 6 Bundesliga matches; add SkillCorner (or others) only if the score still climbs at 6.

Why: realistically about 20 clean-licence matches exist, and each new source costs a reader and a style check.

D25Dead-ball settings: smooth 0.5 s, cut-off 40%, minimum stoppage 4 s, fill play gaps under 1 s

Location clues (#2) stay out of the detector for now and are reconsidered in the match-flow layer.

Why: these settings won independently on all 7 folds of nested leave-one-match-out (+0.012 spellF1). Location clues showed no measurable gain on the bench, which under-represents the blackout–stoppage link.

D26Team frame judge replaces the baseline's team guess

Gradient-boosted trees on team clues (nearest player sustained, team width, block position and movement, speeds), trained on live Bundesliga frames. Interim smoothing: confidence averaged over 2 s, until the match-flow referee (8b) takes over.

Why: +0.14 spellF1 on the bench (0.290 → 0.429), and its clues behave the same on our match (D23 check passed).

D27Match-flow referee replaces the 2-second averaging

4-state HMM with Viterbi; switch 10, stop 160, restart costs 5 and 5, team-model trust 2.0 (stored in analysis/referee.py).

Why: +0.020 spellF1 on the bench (0.429 → 0.449), chosen by all 7 folds of nested leave-one-match-out; a third round confirmed a plateau.

D28Optimise spellF1, but never at macroF1's expense without a clear spellF1 gain

spellF1 ranks; macroF1 breaks ties and is a sanity check. A change that raises spellF1 within noise while dropping macroF1 is rejected.

Why: the ranking rule, plus protection against fooling ourselves with noise-sized spellF1 gains.

D29Turnover clues join the team judge

Close control, control before and after, last and next controller, reception, shape flip.

Why: +0.020 spellF1 on the realistic (jittered) bench at both noise levels tested, with macroF1 up too.

D30No separate change detector in the referee

The referee keeps one fixed switch cost (D27).

Why: per-frame "change moments" added nothing once the team judge had turnover clues (0.488 vs 0.489). Simpler wins.

D31The bench includes 1 m of position jitter from now on

Smooth metre-scale wobble on ball and players (BENCH_JITTER in analysis/bench.py). Every later idea is judged on this realistic bench.

Why: 1 m matches the close-control share of our tracking (tracking-only check), and clean positions had inflated the turnover gain from +0.020 to +0.040.

D32Always keep a valid, reproducible submission in hand

make_submission.py regenerates the file; every improvement must keep it valid and byte-reproducible before it replaces the previous file.

Why: a strong entry is secured 11 days before the deadline; later work is upside, not risk.

D33The public 5-minute score is an alignment check, recorded once

Used to confirm frames and home/away are right. Never used to choose between versions or settings.

Why: the rules, and Step 3 showed those minutes are the easiest of the match.

D34Touch-event clues are not used; stop chasing interceptions for now

The team judge keeps team and turnover clues only (D29).

Why: no measurable gain on the realistic bench (0.449 vs 0.450); 39% of the missed turnovers have no ball at all; further pushing risks fitting the Bundesliga's touch-style labels.

D35Train on control-style labels (Bundesliga labels converted)

Deflection-type opponent spells without control are merged back before training (control_style_labels(); 1 m, 0.4 s, 3 s fixed in advance).

Why: the challenge and the industry standard define possession by control, while DFL's labels are touch-based. Your call, 6 Nov. Effect on our real match cannot be measured on the bench; documented as a principled judgment.

D36No timing shift; keep today's model size

Labels are not shifted; the team judge stays at 300 trees, learning rate 0.1.

Why: we are on time (median +1 frame) and the shift gain was noise (+0.002); bigger models gave no gain.

D37Revises D35: control-style labels become a second scoreboard, not training labels agreed 6 Nov

Keep training on DFL labels as published. Report every change on both scoreboards (DFL labels and control-style labels) and accept it only if neither drops.

Why: training on converted labels gave +0.008 on control-style (noise) but −0.027 on DFL, by under-predicting possessions. The current model already scores 0.517 on control-style labels.

Why: +0.024 spellF1 and +0.042 dead-ball F1 on Metrica (identical games); blackout lengths now match our match better. External data only; organisers confirmed making external data resemble real tracking.

Why: X inside possessions leaves spellF1 unchanged and costs 0.02 macroF1; X on doubtful short pieces gained only 0.0015 (noise) because such pieces are rare.

Rulebook

The football rules our algorithm implements, agreed in Step 5. Source file: docs/possession_rules.md. Each rule becomes a piece of logic.

R1Three states, always exactly one

H, A or D on every graded frame. A loose ball belongs to the last team in control.

R2When the ball goes dead

Out of play, goal, or referee stoppage. For fouls, dead from the moment of the foul (D17). Detected by combining evidence: ball outside the lines, ball slow, players walking, long ball blackout, Smart Tagging set piece.

Stoppages are never brief (0% under 2 s in our 5 min, 1% in the Bundesliga), so a one-second dead blip is noise.

R3Restarts and who gets the ball

Throw-in: team that didn't touch it last (ball at touchline). Corner: attackers (corner flag). Goal kick: defenders (6-yard box). Free kick: team fouled (ball still, wall). Kick-off: team that conceded (centre spot). Type from Smart Tagging, or from ball geometry when tagging misses it.

Throw-ins: combine the player at the touchline with "the team that didn't have it before" (D19). Fallback: restart goes to the other team 70–75% of the time.

Goals: a long ball blackout that ends with the ball on the centre spot is a goal stoppage, so the whole blackout is dead, and the team that conceded (opposite of the team in possession before) kicks off. Smart Tagging kick-off tags fire about 3 s before the ball reappears. Written as a general rule, never as specific frames.

R4Open-play changes need control

An opponent receives the ball and makes a deliberate pass or carry. However short, a spell with a deliberate action counts (D16); a ricochet doesn't. Touches, deflections, 50/50s and aerial duels without control don't change possession; loose balls stay with the last team in control.

R5When the ball is missing

Same team before and after: they kept it. Players walking: probably a stoppage. Different team after: place the change with team shape and tagging phases. Unsure frames: both versions were built and compared (D18); always guessing won (D40).

R6Distrust bad tracking

Ignore impossible ball jumps; reason about teams, never individual players; ball speed from positions.

R7Stay inside the competition rules

Kick-offs and half starts detected by the algorithm, never typed in from the video. Same settings everywhere; tune only on external data.

Glossary

Every technical term, in plain language. ★ marks the ones that decide the score.

No term matches that search.

The basic building blocks

Frame
One still image of the video. 25 frames per second, so 1,500 frames is a minute. The match is 199,319 frames and we label every one.
Label
Our answer for one frame: H Barça have it, A Athletic have it, D ball dead, X we decline to answer.
Graded frames
The frames that are scored: the two halves, 158,857 frames. Pre-match, half-time and post-match are ignored.
Ground truth (GT)
The correct answers we are scored against, like the referee's notebook. Built from human-corrected tracking. We see only the first 5 minutes.
Possession / spell / segment
A segment is any unbroken run of one label. A possession (the scorer says "spell") is a run of H or of A. H H D D H H is two possessions: the throw-in in the middle ends the first one.
Transition / hand-over
The moment possession switches team: a tackle won, an interception, a misplaced pass collected. The hardest and most important frames.
Dead ball
Ball not in play: out of bounds, a foul, offside, a goal celebration, before kickoff. Label D. We never say who "owns" a dead ball.

How we are scored

Precision
Of the things I claimed, how many were right? A striker who scores 3 from 10 shots has 30% precision.
Recall
Of the things that really happened, how many did I catch? A keeper who saves 7 of 10 shots on target has 70% recall.
F1 score
One number combining precision and recall: 2 × P × R ÷ (P + R). High only when both are high, so you can't game it by being cautious or reckless. 0 to 1.
Per-class F1
F1 computed for one label at a time, e.g. "how good am I at saying H".
macroF1★
The tie-breaker. The average of the F1 for H, A and D, computed frame by frame. Roughly "how many minutes did I label right". Weakness: one flickering frame barely hurts it, even though it invents two fake possessions. Floor about 0.16.
IoU (temporal)
Intersection over Union: how well two time intervals overlap. Real possession frames 100–200, mine 120–220: 80 shared ÷ 120 covered = 0.67.
Threshold
A cut-off. "Matched at IoU 0.5" means the overlap must be at least 0.5 to count as the same possession.
spellF1★
The ranking metric. Cut real and predicted labels into possessions; pair each prediction with at most one real possession of the same team, best overlap first; a pair is a hit above the IoU threshold; take F1; average over thresholds 0.3 to 0.7. "Did I find each real possession, with roughly the right start and end, without inventing fake ones?"
Flickering★
Our answer switching between teams for a few frames when nothing changed on the pitch. An Athletic player jogs past the Barça player on the ball, is 10 cm closer for 3 frames, and "nearest player" hands Athletic the ball: H H H A A H H turns 1 possession into 3. Cheap on macroF1, very expensive on spellF1. The trick is telling a real brief win of the ball from a player jogging past.
Fragmentation
Breaking one real possession into several predicted ones. The number-one enemy of spellF1: one wrong frame turns 1 possession into 3.
Abstention (X) and forward-fill★
macroF1 counts X as wrong. spellF1 first replaces each X with your previous label. So X inside a possession is free; X across a hand-over drags the old team past the change.
Confusion matrix
A table with the true answer on each row and what we said on each column. The diagonal is what we got right; everything else shows exactly which mistake we make. Row "truly D", column "we said H" = dead balls we called Barça possession.
Support
How many true examples of a class there are. In the public 5 minutes: H 3,454 frames, A 2,577, D 1,469.
Kappa
Accuracy corrected for lucky guessing. 0 means no better than chance, 1 is perfect.
transF1 / latency
transF1: F1 for catching the moments possession changes, within ±1 second. Latency: how many frames early (−) or late (+) our changes land.
Team accuracy
When the ball is truly alive, how often we name the right team. Measures "who" without "when".
Share error
How far off our overall possession percentage is. The real public 5 minutes are 57% Barça.
Median / percentile (p90)
Sort all values; the median is the middle one, p90 the one 90% of the way up. "Gap median 10.7 s" means half of all ball blackouts are longer than 10.7 seconds. Less fooled by extreme values than an average.
Noise band
Differences too small to mean anything. For spellF1, gaps under about 0.04 on the full match.
Bootstrap
Resampling the possessions many times and rescoring to see how much a score wobbles by luck. If two systems' ranges overlap, neither is clearly better.

The data we have

Tracking data (ATD)
Automatic Tracking Data: position and speed of each detected player and the ball, every frame, produced by software watching the video. Nobody cleaned it.
Normalised coordinates
Positions from 0 to 1 instead of metres. Multiply by 105.3 (length) and 68 (width) to get metres.
Track slot / tracklet
The tracker's numbered lanes, Track 1 to Track 46. A slot is "some player I'm following right now", not a fixed person. We do know each slot's team.
Identity switch
When the tracker swaps two players' slots, e.g. after they cross.
NaN
"Not a Number", the value for missing. The ball is NaN on 41% of graded frames.
Homography
A 3×3 table of numbers per frame converting between a point on the pitch and its pixel in the video.
Reprojection error
How badly a homography misses. Lower is more trustworthy.
Smart Tagging
Metrica's automatic labels for tactical phases (Build Up, Set Pieces, Transitions…), each with times and usually a team. Hints, not answers.
Tactical vs broadcast
Tactical: wide fixed camera, our reference timeline. Broadcast: the TV feed with cuts and replays, on a different timeline. Optional.

ML and modelling words

Algorithm / model
A recipe that turns inputs (tracking) into outputs (labels). A model usually has numbers learned from data; an algorithm can be hand-written rules.
Rule-based / heuristic
Hand-written logic from understanding the game. "If the ball leaves the pitch lines, it's dead."
Feature
A number computed from raw data that helps the decision. Distance from ball to nearest Barça player; average speed of all 22 players.
Baseline
The simplest reasonable solution, used as a yardstick. Here: nearest player to the ball, then smoothing. spellF1 0.2433.
Smoothing / majority vote
Fixing flicker by looking at neighbouring frames. A ±50 window looks 2 seconds either side and takes the most common label.
Hyperparameter
A setting you choose rather than the model learning it, e.g. the smoothing window size.
Tuning
Trying different hyperparameter values and keeping the best-scoring one.
Fitting / calibrating
Adjusting a model's numbers so its outputs line up with reference data. Not allowed on the ground truth or the video.
External data
Data about other matches that we have the licence to use. The only data we may train or tune on.
Licence (CC-BY, MIT)
The legal terms for using a dataset. CC-BY 4.0: use freely, but credit the source. MIT: use freely, keep the copyright notice. No licence at all means unclear rights, so ask.
Domain gap
The difference between the data you tune on and the data you're tested on. Like preseason friendlies on perfect pitches before a season of muddy away games. Ours: clean external tracking vs our messy automatic tracking.
Data degradation
Deliberately making clean data messy (e.g. deleting the ball in long blackouts) so it resembles the data we'll face. Shrinks the domain gap.
Overfitting
Getting very good at the examples you checked and worse at everything else. Like drilling set pieces against one opponent's exact setup.
Training / validation / test
Training: examples a model learns from. Validation: examples used to choose between versions. Test: the final exam. The full match is our test.
Leave-one-match-out
Tune on all matches but one, score on the one left out, rotate so every match gets a turn, then average. Like judging a manager's system on matches they didn't prepare for. A form of cross-validation.
Checksum
A fingerprint of a file. If our download's fingerprint matches the publisher's, the file arrived complete and unchanged.
Supervised / unsupervised
Supervised: learning from examples with answers attached. Unsupervised: finding patterns without answers.
Pseudo-labels
Labels produced by an algorithm, then used to train another model. Allowed if no human decided any of them.
Gradient-boosted trees
A model made of hundreds of small yes/no question trees ("is the nearest Athletic player within 1.5 m?"). Each new tree fixes the mistakes of the ones before; their votes add up to a probability. Strong on tables of features, fast, and explainable.
Probability
How likely something is, from 0 to 1 (or 0–100%). The frame judge says e.g. "H 70%, A 20%, D 10%" instead of a single hard answer, so the referee layer can weigh how sure it is.
Test bench
Our scoreboard: 14 Bundesliga halves made messy like our match, scored by the official metric code. Every idea is judged here.
Learning curve
The score plotted against how much training data the model had. Still rising: more data would help. Flat: spend the effort elsewhere.
Data artefact / shortcut
A pattern caused by how a dataset was made, not by football. The Bundesliga data freezes the ball during stoppages; a model that learns "frozen ball = dead" fails on real tracking.
Permutation importance
How much a clue matters: shuffle that one clue's values and see how far the score drops. A big drop means the model leans on it.
Nested leave-one-match-out
Choosing settings without peeking at the test match: for each match left out, the settings are picked on the other six only, then applied to it. Keeps the reported score honest.
Team shape (width, block)
How spread out a team is (width = spread across the pitch) and where its players sit on average (block). In possession a team stretches wide; out of possession it gets compact. Our top clue for "which team" because it works even when the ball is missing.
Over- / under-segmenting
Predicting too many possessions (flicker) or too few (merging real short ones). spellF1 punishes both.
Jitter / measurement noise
Small random wobble in tracked positions. Real automatic tracking places a player or the ball about a metre off, so "a player within 1.5 m" is less reliable than in clean data.
Stress test
Deliberately making the test harder (here, adding jitter) to see whether a gain survives. If it shrinks to nothing, it was an artefact of clean data.
Ceiling
The best score a part of the system could ever give, measured by plugging in the true answer for that part (on external data only).
Random seed
A fixed starting number for anything random (like where the fake blackouts go), so the result is identical every run.
State model / HMM
Hidden Markov Model: assumes the match is always in one hidden state (Barça ball, Athletic ball, dead) and that states persist; switching has a cost. A natural fit for long possessions and rare switches.
Viterbi algorithm
The standard way to find the most believable sequence of states in an HMM.
Interpolation
Filling a gap with a line between the known points either side, e.g. the ball between two sightings.
Library (numpy, pandas)
Ready-made code we import instead of writing ourselves. numpy does fast maths on big arrays of numbers; pandas works with tables, like a programmable spreadsheet.
Cache
A saved, quick-loading copy of a slow result. We parse the 196 MB tracking file once and keep the result in data/cache/ (git-ignored).
Virtual environment (.venv)
A private box of Python libraries for this project only, so versions don't clash with anything else on the laptop. Git ignores it.
Deterministic / reproducible
Same code plus same data gives exactly the same file every time. Required: the organisers may rerun our repo.
